FROM DANN

I don't know about you, but there's something incredibly satisfying about getting home, taking a shower, putting on my favorite pajamas, and knowing I don't have to go anywhere else. No plans, no getting dressed again, no one expecting me to look presentable. Just me, a good book, and hopefully enough time to read more than three chapters before falling asleep. And honestly, some of my favorite days are the ones when I get to stay in my pajamas a little longer than I probably should.

But here's something I've started paying more attention to: what those pajamas are actually made of. We spend so much time thinking about what we wear when we go out, yet barely give a second thought to the fabrics touching our skin for hours at home. Cotton, linen, silk, bamboo... I used to think choosing something soft was enough, but there's a lot more behind those labels than I realized. So this edit isn't just about finding something cute and comfortable at half off. It's also about understanding what we're wearing, because a little knowledge makes staying in feel even better.

The name on the tag tells you less than the fiber on the label. Here is what is in these pieces, and what to know about the fabrics people ask about most.

Select a name to explore
Cotton+

Cotton is a plant fiber, and it is the most common fabric in this edit. Eczema UK, a charity for people with eczema, says cotton (preferably 100%) tends to be the most commonly recommended textile for people with eczema. It describes cotton as soft, cool, great at absorbing sweat, easily washable and able to let the skin breathe. It also warns that "cotton rich" blends can contain a significant proportion of polyester, so read the label to see how much cotton is actually in the piece.

About the thread: cotton fibers come in different lengths. Combing is a spinning step that removes the shortest fibers. A textile engineering course on NPTEL explains that very short fibers add hairiness to the yarn without adding strength. Fabric made from combed cotton has a softer touch. It also costs more, because combing adds a step and removes some fiber as waste.

Linen+

Linen is a plant fiber, in the same family of vegetable fibers as cotton and hemp. The best research we found on it concerns bedding, not pajamas. A 2024 systematic review in the Journal of Sleep Research found that linen bedsheets improved sleep quality under warm conditions in young adults.

Bamboo+

Be careful with this one. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission explains that when bamboo is processed into fabric advertised as soft, silky or cool to the touch, it has likely been turned into rayon or viscose using toxic chemicals. There is no trace of the original plant left. The FTC adds that there is no evidence that rayon made from processed bamboo is naturally antibacterial. Companies that claim otherwise should have reliable scientific evidence. Eczema UK also notes that bamboo clothing is generally described as "bamboo viscose".

Hemp+

Hemp is a coarse, durable bast fiber. The American Association of Textile Chemists and Colorists (AATCC) says it has high tensile strength and high porosity, which leads to breathability. It also needs less water and pesticides than cotton to grow. These are properties of the fiber, not results from sleep studies.

Silk+

Silk is a protein fiber. Britannica explains that the silkworm Bombyx mori makes a continuous filament from a protein called fibroin. A gummy substance called sericin holds it together and is removed in a step called degumming. Britannica lists strength, elasticity and moisture absorbency among silk's properties. We did not find studies on silk sleepwear and sleep quality, so we make no sleep claims for it. Silk is a fiber, while satin is a weave. Britannica notes that satin was originally made of silk and is now made from yarns of other fibers too.

Lyocell (TENCEL)+

Lyocell is a regenerated cellulose fiber. In U.S. rules, lyocell is not a separate category. Under the Federal Trade Commission's textile rules, rayon is a manufactured fiber made of regenerated cellulose. The same rules allow the name lyocell when the cellulose comes from an organic solution, with no substitution of the hydroxyl groups and no chemical intermediates. In plain words, lyocell sits inside the rayon family and has its own name. TENCEL™ is a brand of fibers made by Lenzing that includes lyocell and modal, according to the brand's own site. Lyocell is the generic fiber name and TENCEL™ is the brand, so not every lyocell or modal fabric is TENCEL™.

Rayon (what is in one of these pieces)+

The Ekouaer button down set is listed as 95% rayon. Under the same federal rules, rayon is a manufactured fiber made of regenerated cellulose. That is the same family the FTC describes when it explains "bamboo" fabrics.

What the sleep research says

A 2024 systematic review in the Journal of Sleep Research looked at the studies on how sleepwear and bedding fibers affect sleep. It found the relationship is complex. Wool sleepwear helped sleep onset in adults in cool conditions and in older adults in warm conditions. Linen bedsheets improved sleep quality in warm conditions for young adults. The review studied cotton, polyester, wool and blended sleepwear. It did not cover silk, bamboo, hemp or lyocell. Two of the authors reported receiving funding from Australian Wool Innovation. The review calls for more standardized research. The honest answer: no single fabric wins for everyone.

Labels worth knowing

OEKO-TEX STANDARD 100 is a label for textiles tested for harmful substances. The certifier says every thread, button and accessory is tested against a long list of harmful substances. The more intensive the skin contact, the stricter the requirements. It is a label to look for on any piece, whatever the fiber.

Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best fabric for sleeping?

There is no single winner. The best research we found says the link between fiber and sleep is complex, and it did not study every fabric. Eczema UK says cotton is the textile most often recommended for people with eczema, and comfort and fit matter as much as fiber.

Why do some listings not say what the fabric is?

Some marketplace listings describe the knit (waffle, ribbed) without giving the fiber percentage. Check the label or the seller's page for the exact piece.

Why is there no price in the text?

Prices and discounts change, so they appear only on the live card next to each piece.
