01 / SUMMARY

Key Takeaways

01

Existentialists say we have no fixed nature at birth; we become who we are by choosing.

02

Nihilism is not one idea: it questions meaning, morality, knowledge or politics, in different ways.

03

Camus rejected the existentialist label and answered the absurd with lucidity and revolt, not despair.

04

None of these ideas asks you to feel empty; each asks you to look at how you live.

PERSONAL NOTE / FROM DANN

Why this edit exists.

Dear reader,

I keep a long note on my phone just for books, ideas, and questions that cross my mind, and somehow the philosophy section never stops growing. I've always been fascinated by questions that don't necessarily have answers. What makes us who we are? How much of what we believe is truly ours, and how much have we inherited from the world around us?

I think what draws me to philosophy is the possibility that things may not be exactly as we perceive them. That there might be more to our existence, our consciousness, and the reality we experience than we currently understand. And maybe what fascinates me most isn't just questioning the world around me, but questioning myself, my own beliefs, even the things I once considered absolute truths.

I don't read philosophy expecting to find all the answers. In fact, some of my favorite books have left me with more questions than I had when I started, and I love that. There's something beautiful about letting yourself be intellectually uncomfortable, and about accepting that changing your perspective doesn't mean losing who you are.

These five come straight out of that note, and the note keeps growing. So will this series, with more books and more questions along the way.

Five books, and counting Dann

03 / CORE FRAMEWORKS

Three ways to question existence

We are not born with a fixed purpose. We become who we are through what we choose, and we answer for it.

Who do I choose to become?
Principal thinkers
Luplau Janssen, public domain
Kierkegaard

Anxiety, faith and the single individual; a religious existentialist who helped lay the groundwork.

Flickr 69061470@N05, CC BY 3.0
Sartre

Radical freedom, responsibility and bad faith; a self-declared existentialist.

Bettmann Archive, public domain
Beauvoir

Freedom within real social situations, and the ethics of living among others.

Willy Pragher, CC BY-SA 3.0
Heidegger

Being and Time (1927) first tied human essence to existence and explored authenticity.

Essential concepts

Freedom

The capacity to choose within real circumstances. Freedom is limited, never absent.

Responsibility

With no excuses behind us and no justifications before us, every choice is ours to own.

Authenticity

Living by your own commitments instead of conforming automatically to social expectations.

Anguish

The unease that comes with freedom: choosing without guarantees.

Existence before essence

No pre-given human nature defines us. We exist first, then define ourselves through action.

Bad faith

Denying either your freedom or your circumstances to escape the weight of choosing.

Defining phrase
"There are no excuses behind us nor justifications before us."
Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, 1946
In everyday life

You chose a career because your family expected it. Existentialism asks whether you can still choose it today, knowing it is yours.

Myth vs reality

Myth: All existentialists are atheists.

Reality: The tradition includes both secular and religious thinkers, from Kierkegaard to Sartre.

Final question

Are you living the life you chose, or the one you were taught to choose?

Nihilism asks whether meaning, morality or knowledge have any foundation at all, and what happens when the old foundations give way.

What remains when inherited meaning collapses?
Principal thinkers
Gustav-Adolf Schultze, public domain
Nietzsche

Diagnosed nihilism as a crisis of values after the death of God; sought to overcome it.

A. M. Gruzdynsh, public domain
Turgenev

His novel Fathers and Sons (1862) popularized the word nihilism through the character Bazarov.

Essential concepts

Existential

Life has no intrinsic meaning or value. This is the most common sense of the word today.

Moral

There are no absolute moral values; good and evil rest on social and emotional pressures.

Epistemological

Knowledge and truth are impossible. This view is linked to postmodern antifoundationalism.

Political

The existing political, social and religious order must be destroyed before any improvement.

Defining phrase
"God is dead."
Nietzsche, The Gay Science, §125

The Gay Science (1882): here "gay" means joyful. Section 125 is a short passage called "The Madman."

In everyday life

You were taught to study, succeed and acquire. Nihilism asks whether those goals carry any meaning of their own, or only the meaning we give them.

Myth vs reality

Myth: Nietzsche believed that nothing matters.

Reality: He treated nihilism as a crisis to overcome, and "God is dead" as a diagnosis, not a celebration.

Final question

If everything you were taught lost its authority, what would you still believe?

The absurd is the clash between our hunger for meaning and a silent world. Camus answers with lucidity, not escape.

How do I live honestly in a world that offers no answers?
Principal thinkers
United Press International, public domain
Camus

The Myth of Sisyphus (1942) made the absurd a way of life: revolt, freedom and lucidity.

Essential concepts

Lucidity

The absurd is lucid reason noting its limits: seeing the human condition without consoling illusions.

Revolt

Refusing both suicide and false hope; living without escape and in defiance.

Passion

Sisyphus's scorn of the gods, hatred of death and passion for life.

Facing fate

Living with the certainty of a crushing fate, without the resignation that ought to accompany it.

Defining phrase
"One must imagine Sisyphus happy."
Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, 1942
In everyday life

Alarm, work, dinner, sleep, repeat. Camus would not ask you to escape the routine, only to see it clearly and still live it fully.

Myth vs reality

Myth: Absurdism means giving up.

Reality: Camus rejects both suicide and false hope; he proposes lucid revolt and a life unreconciled to death.

Final question

Can you love life without ever discovering its ultimate purpose?

05 / OVERVIEW

Three Philosophies Compared

Dimension Existentialism Nihilism Absurdism
Core premise Existence comes first; we define ourselves by acting. Meaning, values or knowledge lack a firm foundation. Human longing for meaning meets a silent world.
View of meaning Created through choices and commitments. Not built into the world. Absent, yet the search for it cannot be switched off.
Response to doubt Choose, commit and take responsibility. Question foundations; Nietzsche asks how to overcome the crisis. Stay lucid and revolt against resignation.
Daily attitude Ask whether your life is truly your own. Check which inherited goals actually carry weight. Live fully, even inside repetition.
06 / TERMS

The Language of Philosophy

Living by your own commitments, willing to break with convention instead of conforming by default.
Denying either your freedom or your circumstances, for example by pretending you have no choice.
The unease that comes with freedom: choosing without guarantees and knowing the choice is yours.
Nietzsche's way of saying belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable, shaking the values built on it.
Nietzsche's ideal of a person who creates values rather than inheriting them, often translated as Overman.
Nietzsche's thought experiment: what if you had to live your life again, identically, forever?
The clash between our need for meaning and a world that gives no clear answer, as Camus describes it.
Camus's response to the absurd: refuse both suicide and false hope, and live without escape, in defiance.
07 / QUESTIONS

FAQ

Q01

What is the difference between existentialism, nihilism and absurdism?

Existentialism says we create ourselves through choices. Nihilism questions whether meaning, values or knowledge have any foundation. Absurdism, Camus's view, accepts the clash between our need for meaning and a silent world.

Q02

Can an existentialist believe in God?

Yes. The tradition includes religious and secular thinkers. Kierkegaard wrote from a Christian perspective, while Sartre was an atheist.

Q03

Was Nietzsche actually a nihilist?

Not in the usual sense. He diagnosed nihilism as a looming crisis of values and looked for ways to overcome it.

Q04

Does absurdism mean nothing matters?

No. Camus rejects despair and false hope alike. The absurd asks us to live lucidly, in revolt, and to find fullness in the struggle itself.

Q05

Do I need to know philosophy before reading these books?

No. Start with Sophie's World, a novel that doubles as a history of philosophy, then follow whichever question stays with you.

Q06

Which book should I start with?

Sophie's World for a first doorway, Zarathustra if you question inherited values, and The Brothers Karamazov for faith and morality.
