Acrylic, Gel or Paint: Which One Fits You

A French tip is a pale, natural-looking nail with a defined band of color across the free edge. You can get it at home with acrylic, with gel, or by painting the line yourself. Painting is the cheapest and easiest to start with, gel gives you a glossy, long-wearing finish, and acrylic builds the most length. Here is how to pick a shape, prep, and keep your nails happy with each method.

MethodWhat sets itSkill levelHow it comes offGood for
PaintRegular polish air-driesBeginnerRegular polish removerA low-cost first try, quick redos
GelCured under a UV or LED lampBeginner to intermediateAcetone soakA glossy finish and color designs
AcrylicA liquid and powder mix that hardens on the nailIntermediateAcetone soak or filingAdding length

New to doing your own nails? Start with paint. Want color and shine that lasts? Try gel. Want extra length and ready to practice? Try acrylic.

Which Nail Shape Suits a French Tip

The shape sets how hard the tip line is to draw and how the finished look reads on your hand.

ShapeWhat to know
OvalAn elongated round with a rounded tip. It flatters shorter fingers and gives the French a soft, classic feel.
SquareA straight free edge and sharp corners. The original French shape, and the easiest to do freehand because the tip line is a single straight swipe.
SquovalA square with the corners rounded off. It chips less at the corners and is the most forgiving shape for a first attempt at home.
RoundFollows the curve of your fingertip. It suits short natural nails and a thin micro-French.
AlmondTapers to a rounded peak. It needs length, and the tip line has to follow the curve, so a guide sticker or pre-shaped tips earn their place.
CoffinAlso called ballerina. It tapers to a flat, squared-off tip, needs length, and chips sooner than square or oval.
StilettoComes to a sharp point. The most dramatic French and the least practical, since it catches on everything.
LipstickCut on a slant. A statement shape and a hard one to do at home.

If you plan to paint the line yourself, pick square, squoval, or oval. For almond or coffin, lean on gel or acrylic extensions.

Step 1: Prep the Nail

Prep is the step people skip, and it is the reason a manicure lifts in two days instead of two weeks.

Start dry and bare. Take off any old polish, and if you had gel on, soak it off rather than prying it, which pulls up layers of natural nail. Shape the free edge by filing in one direction. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends an emery board and a smooth, snag-free edge.

For the cuticles, soak your hands for a few minutes, then push the skin back gently with a cuticle pusher. Do not cut the cuticle. The AAD advises against removing it because that can damage the nail, and its gel manicure tips say cutting the cuticle could lead to inflammation and infection.

Finish with a light buff, brush away the dust, and wipe each nail with isopropyl alcohol. Skip hand cream until the polish is on, since oil is what makes product peel.

Route 1: Paint the Tip Yourself

The cheapest route. Lay down two thin coats of a sheer pink or nude and let it set. Then add the white line: a guide sticker gives a clean straight edge, and a thin striping brush follows a curve. Keep the white light, one or two passes, then finish with a top coat and cap the edge.

Route 2: Gel

Gel gives you a glossy, long-wearing manicure and lets you go beyond French. The steps are base coat, color, then top coat, each cured under a lamp for the time on the bottle. Thin layers cure better than thick ones, and capping the free edge with each coat keeps the tip from peeling.

Both looks above were done at home with the same five things: builder gel, base coat, gel polish, top coat, and a lamp. NailKnowledge describes builder gel ("builder in a bottle") as sitting between a soft and a hard gel: strong enough to reinforce natural nails and build very short extensions, while still soaking off.

Keeping Your Nails Happy With Gel

A few easy habits keep your nails and skin in good shape while you enjoy gel.

The lamp: short and simple. The FDA views nail curing lamps as low risk when used as directed by the label and suggests no more than 10 minutes per hand, per session. Dr. Saira George of MD Anderson agrees the risk from nail dryers appears to be low.

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends a broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or higher on your hands before the lamp. Its artificial nails guide adds that an LED light emits lower levels of UV than a UV curing light and cures more quickly.

Give your nails a break. The AAD says to try not to wear nail polish for one to two weeks or longer between manicures, and to moisturize nails and cuticles with petroleum jelly several times a day. Cleveland Clinic dermatologist Dr. Chey Ranasinghe agrees it is important to give your nails a break and suggests gel for a few special occasions a year instead of every two to three weeks.

Listen to your skin. Some people are sensitive to ingredients in gel products. The Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America says a reaction shows up as redness, swelling, or blistering around the nail. If you notice that, take the product off and treat the area with an over-the-counter cortisone cream. It usually clears up in 1 to 3 days, and an allergist can help if it does not.

If gel is not for you. The AAD says traditional polish is a good option if you have recurring nail problems, since acetone is required to remove gel. The painted route above needs no lamp.

Route 3: Acrylic

Acrylic is a good pick when you want extra length. The American Academy of Dermatology says artificial nails can lengthen short nails and make fingers look long and slender. The FDA explains they are made mainly of acrylic polymers, formed by reacting acrylic monomers with polymers. In a kit, that is the liquid and the powder you combine on the nail. A pre-formed French tip skips the freehand smile line.

How acrylic goes on, step by step

Naio Nails, a nail supply brand, says applying acrylic takes time and patience but can be learned with practice. Its basics list: cuticle pusher, file and buffing block, nail prep (dehydrator), acid-free primer, dappen dish, acrylic brush, lint-free wipes, powder, liquid, and top coat. Its steps, in short:

1. PrepGently push the cuticle back, buff the shine off the nail, and remove the dust.Acrylic will not stick to any cuticle left on the nail bed.
2. Tip (optional)Fit a nail tip that sits snugly, glue it, cut it to length, and file the shape.Do not file upwards from underneath the tip, since it can pop off.
3. Dehydrate and primeApply nail prep (dehydrator) to the whole nail bed, then an acid-free nail primer.The dehydrator air dries quickly and leaves the nail matte.

Keeping Your Nails Happy With Acrylic

Acrylic asks a little more care from your natural nails, and a few simple habits make it easy.

  • Know how it sticks. The AAD explains that the natural nail surface is filed until rough so acrylic sticks, which thins the nail. File lightly and let your nails recover between sets.
  • Keep product off your skin. The FDA says to avoid skin contact with the monomer to minimize the chance of an allergic reaction. If you notice redness, swelling, or pain in the nail bed, take the product off and check with a doctor.
  • Read the label. The FDA says methyl methacrylate monomer is still used occasionally in some artificial nail products, and that it removed products with 100 percent methyl methacrylate from the market in the early 1970s after reports of nail damage and contact dermatitis. Ethyl methacrylate is used occasionally in acrylic nails.
  • Plan your touch-ups. The AAD says wearing artificial nails past a few weeks means touch-ups every 2 to 3 weeks, and that frequent touch-ups can damage natural nails. It suggests saving them for special occasions so your nails can repair in between.
  • Leave the cuticles alone, since the AAD says they protect your nails and the surrounding skin from infection, and keep methacrylic acid nail primers, which the FDA says are usually labeled "For Professional Use Only," away from children.

Finish: Seal the Edge

Finish every route with a top coat, and cap the free edge each time by swiping the brush along the very tip of the nail. That thin seal on the edge is what stops the tip from peeling first.

The Cuticle Oil Routine

Use cuticle oil every night, one drop per nail, massaged into the base and sidewalls. It keeps the nail plate flexible so it bends instead of snapping, and stops the cuticle from splitting. Follow every hand wash with hand cream, since water and dish soap dry nails out, and wear gloves for dishes.

Removing It Without Wrecking Your Nails

The AAD's page on removing gel polish at home, featuring dermatologist Dr. Shari Lipner, says to skip picking or filing the gel off. Her steps:

StepActionGood to know
1. Protect the skinRub petroleum jelly on your cuticles and the skin around your nails.Gather 100% acetone, cotton, plastic food wrap, a washcloth, and a towel.
2. Soak the cottonCut cotton to nail size and soak it in 100% acetone.Acetone can damage surfaces, so line your work area with plastic wrap and a towel.
3. WrapPlace the cotton on each nail and wrap it tightly in plastic food wrap.Plastic wrap makes a tighter seal than foil, so the acetone is less likely to drip.
4. WaitLeave the wrap on for 10 minutes.Most or all of the gel polish should be gone when you unwrap.
5. WipeRemove any leftover polish with a washcloth run under warm water.Rub only the nail and avoid the skin around it.
6. AftercareRub petroleum jelly into your cuticles and the skin around your nails every day for seven days.This helps reduce irritation caused by the acetone.

Dr. Lipner cautions against orange sticks, which can injure your nails and cause white spots. No acetone? The AAD's other option is to let the gel grow out and clip your nails once a week.

Removing acrylic

The golden rule: never pry, pull, or bite acrylic off. Healthline, medically reviewed by Cynthia Cobb, DNP, APRN, WHNP-BC, FAANP, notes that removal done incorrectly can lead to nail thinning, dryness, and even infections. The AAD says artificial nails often need an acetone soak or filing to come off, and Healthline calls the acetone soak the most common way at home. You need 100% acetone, a file, cotton balls, foil, and a cuticle pusher.

Healthline says a salon may be the better choice if your acrylics are new or very thick, you see signs of infection (redness, swelling, pus), or you are unsure how to do it safely. L'Oreal Paris also calls professional removal the safest way.

Frequently Asked Questions

What nail shape is best for a French tip at home?

Squoval, a square with softened corners. The tip line is a straight swipe, and the rounded corners chip less than a true square. Almond and coffin curve, so they are easier with gel or acrylic extensions.

How do you get the tip line straight?

Use a French guide sticker for square and squoval shapes: place it where you want the line, paint the white above it, and peel it off before the polish fully dries. For curved shapes, a thin striping brush and a slow hand work better.

Is gel or acrylic gentler on your nails?

The AAD says to choose soak-off gel instead of acrylic to reduce damage. Gel can still cause brittleness, peeling, and cracking, but it is more flexible than acrylic, so your own nails are less likely to crack. Breaks between sets and gentle removal help either way.

Is biotin worth taking for stronger nails?

It may help some people. A 2024 review in Skin Appendage Disorders reports biotin has been shown to improve nail appearance, strength, and brittleness. It also notes biotin can interfere with certain lab tests, including troponin and thyroid tests, so tell your doctor if you take it.
