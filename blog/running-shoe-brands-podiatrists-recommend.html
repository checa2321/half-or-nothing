What Running Shoe Brands Do Podiatrists Recommend?

2026-09-01 · Written by Dann Nazario · 11 min read

If you search for a podiatrist-approved running shoe, you get a hundred lists that all rank shoes by feel. This one ranks the brands by something you can check: how much formal clinical backing each one has. That means the APMA Seal of Acceptance, registration as a medical device, published research, and how often foot specialists actually name the brand when they write about injuries.

Six of the seven brands below carry the APMA Seal of Acceptance. One does not, and earns its place another way.

This is editorial coverage, not medical advice. It is not a substitute for an exam by a podiatrist, especially if you have foot pain, a diagnosed condition, or a history of injury.

How this ranking works

The clearest signal a running shoe brand can have is the American Podiatric Medical Association Seal of Acceptance. A committee of podiatrists reviews and wear-tests each product, and if most of them agree it supports healthy foot function, it goes to the APMA board for approval. It is not a treatment claim, but it is a real bar, and most shoe brands never clear it.

After that comes peer-reviewed evidence on the shoe features a brand is built around, and how often podiatrists and physical therapists point to the brand in clinical writing.

One caveat worth stating up front. The APMA and the American Academy of Podiatric Sports Medicine do not rank brands or hand out a single best pick. Their advice is to match a shoe's features to your own foot, and to see a sports podiatrist if you have pain or a history of injury. Fit beats brand every time.

1. Skechers

Skechers has the most formal validation of any brand here. Many styles in its Arch Fit and GO Walk lines carry the APMA Seal, and the Arch Fit insole is registered with the FDA as a Class I medical device. Skechers says the Arch Fit shape came out of 20 years of data and 120,000 foot scans, a very different starting point from a designer's sketch, even taken as the brand's own claim rather than an independently audited figure.

2. Brooks

Brooks carries the APMA Seal and is one of the two brands named most often in coverage of plantar fasciitis and overpronation. 3. Hoka

Hoka carries the APMA Seal and is built around one idea: a thick, soft midsole with a rockered shape that rolls you through each stride. It's the other brand named most often for heel pain, arthritis, and joint pain, on the logic that taking impact out of the picture helps.

That approach lines up with a 2020 randomized trial of 848 recreational runners published in the American Journal of Sports Medicine. Runners in the stiffer, less cushioned shoe had about 1.5 times the injury risk, and the protective effect of extra cushioning was clearest in lighter runners. Some people do find the tall stack a little unstable for the first few runs.

Which of these also work for standing all day

If your real problem is eight or ten hours on a hard floor, not a 10K, the picks change. New Balance 990 and 1080, the Skechers Arch Fit and GO Walk lines, and the Hoka Bondi are the ones podiatrists point to for long shifts. Look for a firm heel counter, a supportive footbed, and enough room across the toes.

How to choose

  • Heel-to-toe drop. Eight to twelve millimeters suits most people with heel or Achilles pain. Lower drops move load to the calf and forefoot and need a slow transition.
  • Heel counter. Squeeze the back of the shoe. It should resist, not fold flat.
  • Width. Your foot should not hang over the edge of the sole. New Balance runs wide and offers true widths; most other brands have at least a wide option.
  • Miles. Most trainers lose meaningful cushioning between 300 and 500 miles, sooner if you are heavier or run on pavement.
  • Minimalist and zero-drop shoes. A 2013 study in Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise found runners who moved into minimalist shoes over 10 weeks were more likely to develop bone-marrow swelling in the foot, an early stress-injury sign. They can work, but the switch has to be gradual.

Frequently asked questions

Do podiatrists recommend a specific running shoe brand?

Not a single one. The APMA and the American Academy of Podiatric Sports Medicine do not endorse brands, and both say the right shoe depends on your foot shape, gait, and injury history. In podiatry-adjacent coverage, Brooks and Hoka are named most often for pain-related cases, and Asics for guided stability.

What does the APMA Seal of Acceptance mean?

A committee of podiatrists reviews and wear-tests the product. If most of them agree it promotes healthy foot function, a recommendation goes to the APMA board for final approval. It signals that the shoe supports normal foot mechanics. It is not a claim to treat or cure any condition.

Are running shoes good for walking and standing all day?

Yes. A cushioned, supportive running shoe is one of the better choices for long hours on your feet. Walking and lifestyle shoes do not work the other way around, since they are not built for running loads.

What is the best running shoe for plantar fasciitis?

Look for a firm heel counter, real arch support, a moderate heel drop, and solid cushioning. Common picks are the Brooks Ghost and Adrenaline, the Hoka Bondi and Clifton, and the Asics Gel-Kayano. A stubborn or severe case needs a podiatrist, and often a custom orthotic.

Is a zero-drop or minimalist shoe better for your feet?

Not automatically. There is evidence that a forefoot strike lowers impact forces, and there is also evidence that switching too quickly raises stress-injury risk. If you want to try it, change over two to three months, not two weeks.

How often should I replace running shoes?

Most lose meaningful cushioning between 300 and 500 miles. Heavier runners and pavement wear them out faster. New aches that show up without a change in training are often a sign the shoes are done.

Does insurance or Medicare cover running shoes?

Generally no. Therapeutic shoes can be covered for people with diabetes and qualifying foot disease when a doctor prescribes them. Standard running shoes are an out-of-pocket purchase.
