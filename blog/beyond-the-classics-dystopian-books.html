Where does science end and ethics begin?

When Society Makes Your Choices

Let's start with the dystopias that don't need soldiers in the streets.

No tanks.

No obvious villain.

Just a society that is polite, organized, and absolutely sure it knows what is best for you.

I find this kind of dystopia the hardest to shake, because nobody has to be forced.

The Giver Quartet by Lois Lowry

Okay, let's begin with the one that probably sat on your school reading list.

And if it did, I'd really encourage you to read it again.

Because The Giver is about a futuristic world without social ills, seen through a 12-year-old boy who begins to question his seemingly perfect society.

No pain.

No war.

No difficult choices.

And that's exactly what makes it unsettling.

Because someone has to hold the memories that everyone else was spared. Someone has to know what was lost.

Can a society really remove suffering for free? Maybe somebody always pays for the calm, and the question is whether the rest of us ever find out who.

This is a social dystopia, and one of the most readable doors into the genre.

Reading order

Britannica notes that Gathering Blue, Messenger, and Son complete the Giver quartet. The order is The Giver, Gathering Blue, Messenger, and Son. The box below has all four.

The rabbit-hole question

If a society removed every painful choice from your life, would you call that safety or something else?

If this is the kind of question that keeps you awake...
If The Truman Show made you uneasy about a life that looks perfect from the outside, or if Black Mirror keeps asking what we trade for comfort, The Giver is the gentler, quieter way into the same feeling.

Matched by Ally Condie

Imagine a world where the Society has already decided who you will love, what you will do for work, and even when you will die.

And it all looks very polite.

That's the premise of the Matched trilogy. It follows a girl named Cassia as she moves from acceptance to rebellion.

And the moment that sets it off is small. At her Matching ceremony, Cassia sees the face of Xander appear on the screen as her ideal match, and then, for an instant, another face flashes before the screen fades to black, according to Penguin's official discussion guide. The Society calls it a glitch.

Cassia isn't sure it is.

The question here isn't only about control.

It's about whether a feeling can be yours if someone else chose it for you.

We live in a time of recommendations, matches, and algorithms that suggest who we should talk to next. The Society just does it with more paperwork.

Reading order

Matched is book one of three. The trilogy continues with Crossed and then Reached, as listed on the Penguin Random House series page.

The rabbit-hole question

If an algorithm told you who you would love, and it was right, would the love still be yours?

If this is the kind of question that keeps you awake...
If you liked the romantic tension in The Hunger Games, or the arranged-by-the-system feeling of certain Black Mirror episodes, Matched sits right in between.

Crossed by Ally Condie

And if Matched pulls you in, you will want the next one.

Crossed is the second book in the trilogy, and it follows Cassia as she goes to the Outer Provinces in pursuit of Ky, who was taken by the Society and is believed to be facing his death. When she gets there, she finds he has escaped, leaving clues behind.

The story gets bigger, and the world gets less tidy.

Because once you leave the place that was designed to control you, you may start to wonder how much of you was designed too.

That is the question I think makes this middle book worth reading on its own. Rebellion is easy to imagine as a single brave act. It may be much harder when even your idea of freedom was shaped by the system you are leaving.

Reading order

Book two of three. Read Matched first.

The rabbit-hole question

If you leave the system that shaped you, how much of who you are leaves with you?

If this is the kind of question that keeps you awake...
Read it right after Matched, since the story continues directly. If The Hunger Games made you wonder what comes after the rebellion, this is the quieter version of that question.

When Humanity Redesigns Life

Now let's change the kind of fear.

The first group is afraid of what a society does to people.

This one is afraid of what people do to everything else, including themselves.

The MaddAddam Trilogy by Margaret Atwood

This is not the Atwood of The Handmaid's Tale.

Different fear.

In The Handmaid's Tale, the threat is a government that takes control of people.

Here, the threat is what we do to the planet, and what we might engineer next.

Atwood's own site describes MaddAddam as the conclusion that brings together Oryx and Crake and The Year of the Flood, and says the story points toward the endurance of community and love. This is speculative fiction about biotechnology, ethics, and what is left after a catastrophe, so it belongs in a dystopia conversation even though it doesn't look like the classics.

Scholar Mathias Thaler describes the central mechanism of dystopias as warning through extrapolation, building on what he calls the Atwood Principle, which separates speculative fiction from science fiction. That is how this trilogy feels: not a prediction, but a warning that follows today's science to its logical end.

And the question underneath all of it is uncomfortable.

If we can edit life, who decides what counts as an improvement?

I keep coming back to that.

Reading order

MaddAddam is the third book in the trilogy, so this is a series to read in order: Oryx and Crake, then The Year of the Flood, then MaddAddam. The box below has all three, which is why this is such a good find.

The rabbit-hole question

If science could rebuild humanity to be "better," would you trust the people choosing what better means?

If this is the kind of question that keeps you awake...
If Black Mirror episodes about technology left you with ethical dread more than gadget envy, this trilogy is the long version of that feeling. And if The Handmaid's Tale stayed with you, this is a natural next step with the same author.

When Civilization Itself Is Broken

The last group isn't a classic dystopia at all.

I'm including it on purpose.

The Fifth Season by N. K. Jemisin

Quick heads up: this one is a little different.

It is not a classic dystopia.

It is a fantasy novel, set in a world that keeps ending.

But I think it belongs in this conversation, because the question it asks is the same one the dystopias ask: what does a society do to the people it depends on?

The book opens the Broken Earth trilogy, and its publisher notes that it won the Hugo Award for Best Novel. The setting is the Stillness, a land long familiar with catastrophe, where the power of the earth is wielded as a weapon.

And that is where my dystopia radar goes off: who gets to hold that kind of power, and who pays the price for it?

That question stays with me.

Reading order

The Fifth Season is book one of the Broken Earth trilogy, followed by The Obelisk Gate and The Stone Sky.

The rabbit-hole question

What happens to a society that survives only by controlling the people it fears?

If this is the kind of question that keeps you awake...
If The Handmaid's Tale left you thinking about people reduced to their usefulness, this is a very different world asking a very similar question.

The Peripheral by William Gibson

This one is personal, because it's one of my favorites.

Penguin Random House's description gives you the two worlds right away. Flynne Fisher lives in a rural, near-future America where jobs are scarce. Wilf Netherton lives in London seventy-some years later, on the far side of decades of slow-motion apocalypse.

And somehow, those two worlds are connected.

It is the first book of the Jackpot Trilogy, and it comes from the author of Neuromancer, so if you read Part I, this is a natural next door to open.

What does a future owe to the people who live before it?

According to NPR, Newman retells Orwell's novel through the eyes of the protagonist's girlfriend, with approval from the Orwell estate.

Little Eyes by Samanta Schweblin

Would you rather be a "keeper," inviting an unknown observer into your home through the camera eyes of a fuzzy robot animal, or a "dweller," the anonymous person controlling it from the other side?

Utopia by Thomas More

Okay, this one is not a dystopia.

Britannica explains that More published Utopia in 1516 and coined the word, which comes from the Greek for "no place" and refers to an imaginary, perfect world.

That is why I keep it on a dystopia reading list. It is the starting point for the idea of a perfect society, and many dystopias seem to begin by asking what that perfection would cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I read after the classic dystopian books?

It depends on what hooked you. If it was conditioning and comfort, start with The Giver. If it was a system that decides your life, try Matched and Crossed. If it was science and the planet, try the MaddAddam trilogy.

Is The Giver a dystopian book?

Britannica describes The Giver as a novel about a futuristic world without social ills and a 12-year-old boy who questions his seemingly perfect society, and its biography of Lois Lowry calls Gathering Blue, Messenger, and Son part of the dystopian Giver quartet.

Is The Fifth Season a dystopia?

Not in the classic sense. It is a fantasy novel that its publisher says won the Hugo Award for Best Novel, but a world where the power of the earth is wielded as a weapon makes it a natural pick for dystopia readers.
